There's a lot of noise right now about service animals: "fake" vests sold online, customers bringing pets into restaurants, and staff who aren't sure what they're allowed to say. Get it wrong one way and you've violated a customer's civil rights. Get it wrong the other way and you've got a dog jumping on diners. Here's what the law actually says, and how to handle it at the door.

## What Counts as a Service Animal

Under the federal Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), a service animal is a **dog** that has been **individually trained to do work or perform tasks** for a person with a disability, and the task must be directly related to that disability (28 C.F.R. § 36.104). Examples: guiding a person who is blind, alerting to a seizure, pulling a wheelchair, interrupting a psychiatric episode.

What does **not** count:

- **Emotional support animals.** A dog whose only job is to provide comfort is not a service animal under the ADA — even with a doctor's letter.
- **Therapy dogs** and pets.
- **Other species.** Only dogs qualify. (Miniature horses get similar, case-by-case treatment under a separate rule, 28 C.F.R. § 36.302(c)(9).)

There is **no official registry, certificate, ID card, or vest** required by the ADA. Online "registrations" mean nothing legally — which is exactly why you can't ask for them.

## The Two Questions You Can Ask

When it isn't obvious what the dog does, you or your staff may ask **only two questions** (28 C.F.R. § 36.302(c)(6)):

1. **"Is the dog a service animal required because of a disability?"**
2. **"What work or task has the dog been trained to perform?"**

That's it. You **may not**:

- Ask what the person's disability is
- Ask for medical records, certification, ID, or training papers
- Ask the dog to demonstrate its task
- Require a vest or special harness
- Charge a pet deposit, cleaning fee, or surcharge (28 C.F.R. § 36.302(c)(8)) — though you may charge for actual damage if you'd charge any customer for the same damage

If the task is obvious — the dog is guiding someone who is blind, or pulling a wheelchair — don't ask at all.

Washington state law mirrors these same two questions and the same ban on documentation or demonstrations (RCW 49.60.214(2)).

**Staff script:** *"Welcome in! Is that a service animal required because of a disability? Great — and what task is it trained to do for you?"* Then let them proceed. Friendly, consistent, done.

## When You CAN Ask a Service Dog to Leave

Even a legitimate service dog can be excluded if (28 C.F.R. § 36.302(c)(2)):

- **The dog is out of control and the handler doesn't take effective action to control it**, or
- **The dog is not housebroken.**

The dog must be **harnessed, leashed, or tethered** unless that interferes with its work or the person's disability — in which case the handler must keep control by voice, signal, or other effective means (28 C.F.R. § 36.302(c)(4)). You are **not** responsible for caring for or supervising the dog (§ 36.302(c)(5)).

If you remove the dog, you must still offer the person your goods or services **without the animal present** (§ 36.302(c)(3)). Allergies or fear of dogs are **not** valid reasons to refuse service; separate the parties within the space if needed.

The decision must be based on **this dog's actual behavior right now** — not on breed, size, or assumptions about what a service dog "should" look like.

## Service Dogs in Training: What Washington and Iowa Say

The **federal ADA does not cover dogs in training.** Under the ADA, the dog must already be trained before it has public access rights. But many states — including both of ours — do protect trainees.

### Washington (RCW 49.60.215, effective June 6, 2024)

Washington used to be one of only three states without protection for dogs in training. That changed with ESSB 5788 (Laws of 2024, ch. 161).

- A place of public accommodation **may not deny a service animal trainer** the right to be accompanied by a **service animal trainee** in any area open to the public (RCW 49.60.215(6)).
- A "service animal trainee" is a dog (or miniature horse) **undergoing training to become a service animal**; the "trainer" is the person controlling it during a training program (RCW 49.60.040).
- **The trainer must keep control** — generally by harness, leash, or tether (RCW 49.60.215(5)).
- **You may ask a trainee to leave** if it's not housebroken or if it's out of control and the trainer doesn't take effective action.
- **You may charge the trainer for damage** the trainee causes, if you customarily charge customers for damage (RCW 49.60.215(4)).
- **Exception:** the trainee rule does not apply to places that provide **lodging for transient guests** (hotels, motels, short-term rentals).

### Iowa (Iowa Code § 216C.11)

Iowa has protected trainees for years.

- A person with a disability, a person assisting them by controlling the animal, **or a person training a service animal** has the right to be accompanied by a **service animal or service-animal-in-training**, under control, in public places and accommodations.
- **No extra charge** may be required for the animal.
- The handler **is liable for any damage** the animal causes to the premises.
- Knowingly denying or interfering with these rights is a **simple misdemeanor**.

**Bottom line:** in both Washington and Iowa, a properly controlled dog in training gets access to your public areas, and the same "out of control / not housebroken" exclusions apply.

## Faking It: The Penalties

Both states punish people who pass off a pet as a service animal:

- **Washington:** Misrepresenting an animal as a service animal or trainee is a **civil infraction of up to $500** (RCW 49.60.214). A violation occurs when someone represents an animal as a service animal to get access rights and knew or should have known it didn't qualify. **Businesses may post a sign** warning that misrepresentation can result in a civil infraction of up to $500 (RCW 49.60.215(6)(c)). If a person refuses to answer the two questions when asked by an enforcement officer, the law presumes the animal is not a service animal.
- **Iowa:** Intentionally misrepresenting an animal as a service animal or service-animal-in-training to obtain rights under state or federal law is a **simple misdemeanor** (Iowa Code § 216C.11(3)). The legislature updated this offense in 2025.

**Important:** enforcement is for law enforcement, not your front counter. Your job is to ask the two questions, watch behavior, and remove an out-of-control animal — not to accuse anyone of fraud.

## Behaviors to Watch: Trained Service Dog vs. Pet

You can't demand proof, but behavior tells you a lot — and behavior is what the law lets you act on.

| A trained service dog usually... | Red flags (and grounds to act if uncontrolled) |
|---|---|
| Stays close to the handler, focused on them | Wanders, pulls toward people, food, or other dogs |
| Walks calmly on a loose leash | Lunges, drags the handler, constant leash tension |
| Ignores food on tables and floors | Begs, sniffs tables, eats off the floor |
| Settles quietly under a table or at the handler's feet | Can't settle; paces, whines, climbs on furniture or in carts |
| Is quiet; may bark once if alerting or provoked | Repeated or excessive barking, growling, snapping |
| Ignores other customers and other dogs | Jumps on, licks, or mouths people; reacts to other dogs |
| Relieves itself only on command, outside | Urinates or defecates inside |
| Is carried only if the task requires it | Riding in a shopping cart or held like a pet the whole visit |

Two cautions:

- **One bark isn't "out of control."** The DOJ says a single bark, or barking because someone provoked the dog, does not mean the dog is out of control. Repeated barking in a quiet setting can be.
- **Small dogs and "unusual" breeds can be legitimate.** A Chihuahua can be a trained medical alert dog. Judge conduct, not looks.

## A Simple Policy for Your Team

1. **Train every customer-facing employee** on the two questions — word for word.
2. **Never ask** about disability, papers, vests, or demonstrations.
3. **Watch behavior.** If a dog is out of control, give the handler a chance to correct it.
4. **If it continues,** politely ask that the dog be removed — and offer to continue serving the customer without it.
5. **Document the incident** (date, time, behavior observed, what was said).
6. **In Washington,** consider posting the optional misrepresentation sign.
7. **Don't charge pet fees** — only actual damage you'd charge any customer for.

## Your Employees Are a Different Rule

Everything above is about **customers** (ADA Title III). When an **employee** asks to bring a service or support animal to work, that's a **[reasonable accommodation](/blog/reasonable-accommodations-small-business)** request under ADA Title I and state employment law — a different process where you *can* engage in an interactive discussion and, in some cases, request documentation. Don't apply the "two questions only" rule to staff, and don't flatly deny the request without going through the accommodation process.
