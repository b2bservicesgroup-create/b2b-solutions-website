"Should I be an LLC or an S corp?" is one of the most common questions we hear from small business owners. Part of the confusion is that the two aren't really alternatives. An **LLC** is a legal structure you form with the state. An **S corporation** is a tax election you make with the IRS. You can be one, the other, or both.

Here's how the three most common setups compare.

## Sole proprietorship

A sole proprietorship is the default when one person starts doing business without forming an entity. There's nothing to form at the state level, though in Washington you still need a business license, and you may need a trade name if you operate under a name other than your own.

- **Liability:** No separation. Your personal assets (home, savings, vehicles) can be reached for business debts and lawsuits.
- **Taxes:** Business profit goes on Schedule C of your personal return. You pay income tax plus self-employment tax on all of it.
- **Best for:** Testing an idea or running a very small, low-risk side business.

## Limited liability company (LLC)

An LLC is a legal entity you form with the state. Its main job is protection: it puts a wall between your business and personal assets, as long as you keep finances separate and follow the rules.

- **Liability:** Generally protects personal assets from business debts and claims.
- **Taxes:** By default, the IRS treats a single-member LLC exactly like a sole proprietorship (Schedule C, full self-employment tax). Multi-member LLCs file as partnerships.
- **Best for:** Most small businesses that want liability protection with simple tax filing.

## S corporation

An S corp is a tax status, not a type of entity. An LLC (or a corporation) can elect it by filing IRS Form 2553. Once you elect it, you become an employee of your own business.

- **Liability:** Same as the underlying LLC or corporation.
- **Taxes:** You pay yourself a **reasonable salary** through payroll, with Social Security and Medicare withheld. Remaining profit can be taken as distributions, which aren't subject to self-employment or payroll tax. Income tax still applies to all of it.
- **Extra requirements:** Running payroll, quarterly payroll filings, W-2s, a separate business tax return (Form 1120-S), and cleaner bookkeeping.
- **Best for:** Profitable businesses where the payroll tax savings outweigh the added cost and paperwork.

## Example: single-member LLC vs. S corp

Let's say your business earns **$120,000 in net profit** in 2026.

### As a single-member LLC (default tax treatment)

Self-employment tax is 15.3% (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare), applied to 92.35% of net profit:

- $120,000 × 92.35% = $110,820
- $110,820 × 15.3% = **about $16,955 in self-employment tax**

### As an LLC taxed as an S corp

You pay yourself a $60,000 salary and take the remaining $60,000 as distributions:

- Payroll taxes on salary: $60,000 × 15.3% (employee + employer share) = **$9,180**
- Payroll tax on the $60,000 in distributions: **$0**

**The difference:** about **$7,775** less in Social Security and Medicare tax.

### But that's not the whole picture

From that savings, subtract the cost of running an S corp:

- Payroll processing
- A separate business tax return
- State payroll obligations such as unemployment insurance, workers' comp (L&I) and paid family leave
- More bookkeeping and year-end work

These costs can easily eat a meaningful share of the savings, especially at lower profit levels. Lower Social Security wages can also mean a smaller Social Security benefit in retirement, and an S corp salary can change how much of the qualified business income (QBI) deduction you get.

**About "reasonable salary":** The IRS requires S corp owners who work in the business to pay themselves a salary comparable to what they'd pay someone else to do the job. Setting it artificially low to avoid payroll tax is a common audit target. The $60,000 above is for illustration only. Your number depends on your industry, role and location.

## A note for Washington businesses

Washington has no state income tax, so the S corp decision here is mostly about federal payroll taxes. B&O tax is charged on gross receipts no matter which structure you choose, so an S corp election won't change it.

## When does an S corp make sense?

There's no single cutoff, but many business owners find an S corp isn't worth it until profit is steady and comfortably above what a reasonable salary for their role would be. If your profit swings a lot year to year, or you're just starting out, a regular LLC is often the simpler choice.

The election also has deadlines. To take effect for the current year, Form 2553 generally has to be filed within 2 months and 15 days of the start of the tax year (March 15 for most calendar-year businesses), though late-election relief is sometimes available.
