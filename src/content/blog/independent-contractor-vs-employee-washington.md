"Can't I just pay them as a 1099?"

It's one of the most common questions we hear from small business owners. On the surface, an independent contractor looks simpler and cheaper: no payroll taxes, no workers' comp premiums, no paid sick leave, no PFML. Just pay the invoice and move on.

The catch is that **you don't get to choose**. Whether someone is an employee or an independent contractor depends on how the work is actually done, not on what you call them, what the contract says, or which tax form you send in January. And in Washington, the bar for treating someone as a contractor is noticeably higher than the federal one.

Here's how the classification really works, the tests each agency uses, and what it costs when it's wrong.

## Why Classification Matters

When someone is your **employee**, you're responsible for:

- Withholding federal income tax and the employee's share of Social Security and Medicare, and paying the employer's share
- Federal unemployment tax (FUTA)
- Washington unemployment insurance through the Employment Security Department (ESD)
- Workers' compensation premiums through Labor & Industries (L&I)
- Paid Family & Medical Leave and WA Cares premium withholding
- Minimum wage, overtime, paid sick leave, and meal and rest break rules

When someone is a true **independent contractor**, almost none of that applies. They run their own business, pay their own self-employment tax, and carry their own coverage.

That gap is exactly why the agencies look closely, and why "we just called them a contractor" doesn't hold up in an audit.

## The Three Questions Every Owner Should Ask First

Before getting into the legal tests, these questions will tell you a lot:

1. **Who controls how the work gets done?** If you set the hours, train them on your methods, supervise the work, or tell them how to do the job, that points to an employee.
2. **Is this their business, or yours?** A contractor has their own business: their own clients, tools, business license, and the chance to make or lose money. Someone who only works for you, using your equipment, looks like an employee.
3. **Is the work part of what your business does?** A plumbing company hiring a plumber is very different from a plumbing company hiring a web designer for a one-time site rebuild.

If you're answering "me," "mine," and "yes," you're very likely looking at an employee.

## The Federal Test: IRS Common-Law Rules

The IRS looks at the whole relationship, grouped into three categories:

- **Behavioral control**: Do you control, or have the right to control, what the worker does and how they do it?
- **Financial control**: Do you control the business side of the job: how they're paid, whether expenses are reimbursed, who provides the tools?
- **Type of relationship**: Is there a written contract, are benefits provided, is the relationship expected to continue, and is the work a key part of your business?

No single factor decides it. The IRS weighs everything together. If you genuinely can't tell, either party can file **Form SS-8** and ask the IRS for a determination, though it takes time.

## Washington Uses Its Own Tests

This is where many owners get caught. Passing the IRS test doesn't mean you're fine in Washington. The state has separate, stricter tests, and each agency applies its own.

### L&I (Workers' Compensation)

L&I starts with the **personal labor test**. A worker isn't covered by your workers' comp if one of these is true:

- They bring their **own employees** to do the work, and you don't control them or their crew, **or**
- They bring **heavy or costly specialized equipment** and the expertise to run it (think earth-moving equipment), and you don't control them.

If neither is true, which covers most solo contractors, the worker has to pass **all six parts** of L&I's independent contractor test:

1. **Free from your direction and control**, both under the contract and in practice.
2. The work is **outside your usual course of business**, *or* performed **outside all of your places of business**, *or* the contractor is **responsible for the costs of their own principal place of business**.
3. They're **customarily engaged in an independent business** of the same nature, *or* they have a principal place of business that qualifies for an IRS business deduction.
4. They're responsible for **filing a schedule of business expenses with the IRS** (such as a Schedule C).
5. They have an **account with the Department of Revenue** (a UBI number) and other state agencies as required.
6. They keep a **separate set of books or records** for all of their business income and expenses.

**Construction has a seventh part:** the contractor must be properly **registered as a contractor** with L&I, or hold a valid **electrical contractor license**. Registration alone isn't enough, though. They still have to pass the other six.

Miss any single part, and L&I treats the person as your covered worker.

### ESD (Unemployment Insurance)

ESD starts from the assumption that **anyone you pay for services is in covered employment** unless you can prove otherwise. You can show it with either of two tests, and the worker has to pass every part of the one you use:

- **The three-part test:** the worker is free from your control, the work is outside your usual course of business or outside all of your places of business, *and* the worker is customarily engaged in an independent business of the same kind.
- **The alternative six-part test:** essentially the same six parts L&I uses, including the IRS expense schedule, the Department of Revenue account and UBI number, and separate books.

Construction and electrical work also require valid contractor registration or an electrical license.

These cases often start when a former "contractor" files for unemployment. ESD finds no wage records for them, and the audit begins from there.

### Wage and Hour Rules

For minimum wage and overtime under Washington's Minimum Wage Act, courts and L&I look at the **economic reality** of the relationship: is this person really in business for themselves, or are they economically dependent on you? Calling someone a contractor doesn't exempt them from minimum wage or overtime if they're really an employee.

## A Few Common Washington Scenarios

- **The "helper" who works your jobs, on your schedule, with your tools.** Almost certainly an employee. They fail the control test immediately.
- **The cleaner, landscaper, or tradesperson with their own business, license, insurance, and other clients, who sets their own methods.** This can be a legitimate contractor, as long as you document that they pass each part of the test.
- **A former employee who comes back "on 1099" doing the same job.** This is a major red flag. If nothing about the work changed except the tax form, the classification probably didn't change either.
- **A bookkeeper or IT consultant who serves many businesses and invoices you monthly.** Often a valid contractor, especially when the work is outside your core business.

## What Misclassification Costs

If an agency reclassifies a contractor as an employee, the bill can come from several directions at once:

- **L&I**: unpaid workers' comp premiums, plus penalties and interest. Worse, if that worker gets hurt on the job while uncovered, you may face the cost of the claim itself.
- **ESD**: back unemployment taxes on that worker's wages, plus penalties and interest.
- **IRS**: back employment taxes (income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare, and FUTA), plus penalties.
- **Wages**: unpaid overtime, sick leave, and other wage claims under the Minimum Wage Act.
- **PFML and WA Cares**: premiums that should have been withheld, which you generally can't go back and collect from the worker.

These can stack up across several years before anyone notices. And audits rarely stop at one worker: if one contractor is reclassified, expect the agency to look at everyone you've paid the same way.

## A Quick Note on 1099s for 2026

Starting with payments made in **2026**, the federal reporting threshold for **Form 1099-NEC** rose from **$600 to $2,000** per contractor for the year, and it will be adjusted for inflation starting in 2027. That changes *when you have to file a 1099*. It does **not** change *who is an employee*. A worker who should be on payroll is an employee whether you pay them $500 or $50,000.

Collect a **Form W-9** from every contractor before you make the first payment, and track your totals throughout the year so you know who crosses the threshold.

## How to Protect Your Business

If you use independent contractors, do it on purpose and keep the paperwork:

- **Run each worker through the L&I and ESD tests**, and write down how they pass each part. L&I specifically recommends documenting this for every contractor.
- **Get proof** of their UBI number, business license, contractor registration (for construction), and their own insurance.
- **Use a written independent contractor agreement** that matches how the work actually happens. The contract helps, but agencies look at reality over paperwork.
- **Let contractors run their own business**: their own tools, schedule, methods, and invoices. Pay by the project or invoice, not by an hourly timesheet you manage.
- **Re-check when the work changes.** A short project that turns into a full-time, ongoing role often turns into an employee along the way.
- **When in doubt, put them on payroll.** Bringing someone on as a W-2 employee from the start almost always costs less than a reclassification audit later.
